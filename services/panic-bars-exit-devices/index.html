Exit hardware in an industrial building lives a harder life than exit hardware anywhere else. The device on a shipping exit gets hit by carts, clipped by skids, leaned on, and pushed by people whose hands are full — and unlike a restaurant door, nobody is looking at it.

The requirement does not change with the building: one motion from the inside, no key, no thumbturn, no special knowledge. Ontario’s Building Code mandates panic hardware on exit doors serving an assembly floor area with an occupant load over one hundred, which around here covers the banquet halls and venues along Highway 7 and Weston Road, Woodbridge restaurants, places of worship, school gyms, arenas and community centres, plus the assembly and retail space folded into Vaughan Mills and Interchange Way. Plenty of plant exits carry devices anyway, and that is a good instinct.

What comes off our trucks most often for these: vertical rods re-adjusted so the top latch engages again, worn latches and touchpads, bent push rails on otherwise sound chassis, dogging mechanisms, and outside trim seized by a winter of salt. On rated openings we correct permanently dogged devices, because a door that cannot latch is not a separation, and we keep labelled fire exit hardware labelled. Where a door sits on a fork route we will also say plainly whether a bollard belongs in front of it, because the same device will otherwise be back.

FAQs

Panic Bars & Exit Device Service — your questions

A pallet truck has bent the bar on our shipping exit. Repair or replace?

It depends where the damage stopped. A bent touchpad or push rail on an otherwise sound chassis is often a parts change, and that is a good outcome. If the chassis or the mounting took the hit, the device is done. Either way we will look at whether a bollard or a guard belongs in front of that door, because a device on a fork route gets hit twice.

Do warehouse exits need panic hardware, or is that only for assembly buildings?

The occupant-load rule that mandates panic hardware is written around assembly spaces, but plenty of industrial exits carry devices anyway and it is often the right call: a one-motion release on a door people will meet in the dark with their hands full is worth having regardless of the minimum. What matters more in a plant is that whatever is on the door actually works.

The exit device is fine but the door will not shut behind it. Related?

Very often, yes. If the closer cannot drive the door home the latch never engages and the bar takes the blame; and if the latch is binding, the closer gets cranked up to force it, which wears both. We set the closer and the device together as one assembly, because adjusting either alone tends to come undone within weeks.
